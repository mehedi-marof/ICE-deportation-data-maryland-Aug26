Immigration enforcement

ICE's arrests in Maryland nearly doubled this year. The growth is people with no criminal record

Who ICE arrested in Maryland since January 2025, where agents found them and where they went next, traced through the agency's own records.

By Mehedi Marof

Scroll to read↓

From January through July of this year, U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement made 2,952 arrests in Maryland of people with no prior criminal conviction and no pending criminal charge in its records. That is more than in the nearly three years from October 2022 through July 2025 combined.

ICE's arrests in Maryland nearly doubled, from 2,224 in the first seven months of 2025 to 4,182 in the same months of 2026. Arrests of people without a record rose by 2,011, more than the overall increase of 1,958, because arrests of people with records fell slightly. Arrests of people with prior criminal convictions dipped from 859 to 820, and arrests of people facing pending charges from 424 to 410. Most of the growth came from arrests in communities, but Maryland's jails remained a steady route to ICE.

Capital News Service analyzed ICE arrest, detainer and detention records released by the Deportation Data Project, a research group at the University of California, Berkeley, and UCLA that obtained them through a public records lawsuit. The records run from Oct. 1, 2022, through Aug. 6, 2026. Apart from comparisons of the first seven months of each year, this story looks at the period since President Donald Trump began his second term on Jan. 20, 2025.

What "no record" means

ICE sorts the people it arrests by criminal history at the time of arrest: previously convicted of a crime, facing a pending criminal charge, or neither. ICE calls the last group "other immigration violators." They are accused of breaking civil immigration law, such as by entering without permission or overstaying a visa, but have no prior criminal conviction or charge in ICE's systems. It does not mean they were in the country legally. About one in four people with no record whom ICE arrested in Maryland since Jan. 20, 2025, already had a final order of removal from an immigration judge.

Arrests rose across the country too, but differently. Nationwide, ICE made 252,652 arrests from January through July, up about 75% from a year earlier. Arrests of people without records rose the most, but arrests of people with pending charges also rose about 64%, and arrests of people with prior convictions rose about 19%. In Maryland, arrests of people with prior convictions or pending charges dipped slightly.

Since Trump took office, about 63% of the people ICE arrested in Maryland had no record, compared with about 39% nationwide. Among the 43 states and territories with at least 1,000 ICE arrests in that time, only New York and West Virginia had higher shares.

That share has swung before. In late 2022, under President Joe Biden, about three in four people ICE arrested in Maryland had no record. By December 2024, the share had fallen to about 17%. After Trump returned to office, it rose almost every month, to a peak of about 77% in February 2026. Since then it has stayed between about 62% and 72%.

Most people ICE arrested in Maryland are from Central America and Mexico. Since Jan. 20, 2025, about 78% were citizens of Guatemala, El Salvador, Honduras or Mexico.

Jails steady, streets surging

ICE makes arrests in two main ways. In a custodial arrest, a jail or prison hands someone over to the agency. In a non-custodial arrest, agents find the person at home, at work, on the road or at an ICE office.

In Maryland, both routes stayed busy. Non-custodial arrests more than doubled, from 1,598 in the first seven months of 2025 to 3,667 in the same months of 2026. Custodial arrests dipped from 626 to 515. Nationwide, both kinds rose by about three-quarters.

How someone is arrested tracks closely with who is arrested. Since Jan. 20, 2025, about 69% of ICE's community arrests in Maryland involved someone with no record, compared with about 27% of handoffs from jails and prisons.

The jail pipeline is still running

Maryland's jails and prisons remain a steady route into ICE custody. Since the state banned 287(g) agreements in February, ICE has recorded at least 385 arrests of people handed over by jails, prisons and other custody. That is about 69 a month, down only slightly from about 77 a month in the 13 months between President Donald Trump’s inauguration and the ban. And the pipeline does not only catch people with criminal records: about 27% of the people handed to ICE from jails and prisons since Trump took office had no prior conviction or pending charge.

The ban closed one door. Before it, eight Maryland counties had agreements with ICE under the federal 287(g) program. In three of them, Frederick, Harford and Cecil, "jail enforcement" agreements trained local jail officers to check inmates' immigration status. Almost all of the 478 arrests ICE coded as 287(g) since October 2022 came from those three counties. Those arrests made up about 4% of ICE's Maryland arrests in the 13 months before Gov. Wes Moore signed a ban on the agreements on Feb. 17. ICE recorded 11 such arrests from March through July.

The other doors stayed open. ICE keeps asking jails to hand people over through detainers. A detainer is ICE's written request that a jail or prison hold someone for up to 48 hours past their release, or tell the agency when they will be released, so agents can pick them up. It is a request, not an arrest. Since the ban, ICE has lodged at least 664 detainers at Maryland county jails and at least 154 at state prisons and state-run jails. That is about 118 a month at county jails, down from about 142 in the 13 months before the ban, and 27 a month at state facilities, close to the 29 before.

Those requests usually work. Since Jan. 20, 2025, ICE has lodged about 3,300 detainers at Maryland jails and prisons. Of the half with a recorded outcome, about 62% ended with ICE taking the person into custody. The other half have no outcome yet, so the records cannot show whether the Community Trust Act, a state law that took effect in May and limits how local jails respond to ICE, has made a difference. The U.S. Justice Department sued to block the law in July. The records also do not explain why ICE's custodial arrests fell in Maryland while they rose nationwide.

Arrests per 10,000 residentsEastern Shore

The Eastern Shore saw the sharpest change. ICE made 730 arrests of people with no record in the Shore's nine counties from January through July of this year, up from 189 in the same months of 2025. Wicomico County, home to Salisbury, had the highest arrest rate in the state, about 107 per 10,000 residents since Jan. 20, 2025, and almost all of those arrests were made in the community rather than at the county jail. ICE also runs a hold room in Salisbury, where 1,843 people arrested in Maryland since January 2025 were booked first. These rates are measured against everyone who lives in a county, not just immigrants, and Ocean City's summer crowds inflate Worcester County's rate.

Some arrests happen at ICE's own Baltimore offices, including one where immigrants under supervision report for scheduled check-ins. Those arrests peaked at 281 in the first three months of 2026.

After an arrest

Many people first wait in a hold room at ICE's Baltimore field office. ICE's detention standards limit hold room stays to 12 hours, though the agency gave the Baltimore room a waiver, according to Maryland's U.S. senators. In 2023 and 2024, half the people booked there left within about six hours. Among those booked in January 2026, half stayed longer than 106 hours, more than four days.

On March 6, a federal judge ruled that conditions in the hold room violated detainees' constitutional rights and limited how many people it could hold. Among people booked in July, half left within about six hours again.

Maryland has no long-term immigration detention center, so most people are moved, often more than once. ICE detained 8,319 people arrested in Maryland from Jan. 20, 2025, through early August 2026. Its records place at least 14 of them at holding sites at Guantánamo Bay, Cuba.

Most were sent to their own countries.

ICE recorded 6,084 departures. Guatemala, El Salvador, Mexico and Honduras received the most. Circles are sized by recorded departures.

Destination country

271 went somewhere else.

They were sent to a country other than the one ICE lists as their citizenship. 191 went to Mexico, most of them citizens of El Salvador, Guatemala or Honduras; 154 of the 271 had no prior conviction or pending charge. Mexico is often a country people crossed on the way north, so "another country" isn't always a place they'd never been.

A few crossed an ocean.

A handful of West Africans were sent to other West African countries, such as five Nigerians sent to Ghana. Destinations are as ICE recorded them, which can be where a flight landed rather than where someone settled.

The records end in early August, too soon to measure the Community Trust Act. The next release from the Deportation Data Project should show whether ICE's arrests from Maryland jails kept falling, and whether its arrests in communities kept rising.

What the records can't tell us

  • Why ICE arrested someone. The records show criminal history and how an arrest happened, not the reason for it.
  • A complete criminal history. "No record" reflects ICE's systems at the time of arrest, which can be incomplete.
  • Exact locations. ICE often records a general area rather than an address. CNS placed arrests by the city or county ICE listed.
  • Final counts for recent months. ICE updates its records over time, and the latest months may grow.
  • Every 287(g) arrest. Only arrests ICE itself coded as 287(g) are counted as such.

How we did this analysis

The records

This analysis uses U.S. Immigration and Customs Enforcement records that the Deportation Data Project, a research group at the University of California, Berkeley, and UCLA, obtained through Freedom of Information Act lawsuits and published on its data page. We used the project's release of Aug. 24, 2026, which covers Oct. 1, 2022, through Aug. 6, 2026, and four of its files: arrests, detainers, arrests linked to detention stays, and detention stints. In every file, we removed rows the project flags as likely duplicates, using its duplicate_drop_row column.

Arrests

In the arrests file, each row is one ICE arrest. We counted an arrest as a Maryland arrest when the project's apprehension_state_filled_in column says Maryland. That column uses the state ICE recorded and, when ICE left it blank, fills it in from the arrest location; it identifies 12,859 Maryland rows, 214 more than ICE's own state field. After removing 218 likely duplicates, 12,641 arrests remained. Some people were arrested more than once, and 28 rows have no person identifier (unique_identifier), so the story counts arrests rather than people. Each dot on the title screen is one Maryland arrest from January through July 2026: 2,952 red dots for people with no prior conviction or pending charge, and 1,230 gray dots for people with a prior conviction or a pending charge.

Criminal history comes from apprehension_criminality, which records what ICE's systems showed at the time of arrest: "Convicted Criminal," "Pending Criminal Charges" or "Other Immigration Violator." The story calls the last group people with no prior conviction or pending charge. Whether a person already had a final order of removal comes from final_order_before_apprehension.

How an arrest happened comes from apprehension_method_simple. We treated "Custodial Arrest," which includes ICE's Criminal Alien Program, and "287(g) Program" as custodial arrests, meaning a jail or prison handed the person to ICE. All other arrests, mostly those ICE codes "At-Large Arrest," are non-custodial. Arrests at ICE's two Baltimore offices were identified from event_landmark, where ICE lists them as "NON DETAINED ENCOUNTERS AT BAL ERO" and "BALTIMORE ISAP OFFICE."

The story uses two time periods. Year-over-year comparisons use Jan. 1 through July 31 of each year, because the records end Aug. 6. Everything else covers Jan. 20, 2025, when President Donald Trump's second term began, through Aug. 6, 2026, unless the text says otherwise. Comparisons around Maryland's 287(g) ban are monthly averages over periods of different lengths, Jan. 20, 2025, through Feb. 16, 2026 (393 days), and Feb. 17 through Aug. 6, 2026 (171 days): each count was divided by the number of days and multiplied by 30.4375, the average length of a month. National figures come from the same file, using all states. The state comparison includes the 43 states and territories with at least 1,000 arrests since Jan. 20, 2025, and leaves out the District of Columbia.

Places

To place arrests in counties, we first checked whether event_landmark names a county or Baltimore City. If it did not, we used the city ICE recorded in apprehension_city_original, matched to a county through a lookup table CNS built from every spelling in the data. Most spellings matched a ZIP code directory directly; others were corrected by hand or matched to the closest valid spelling, and a few could not be placed. In all, 98.7% of arrests, and 99.5% of those since Jan. 20, 2025, were assigned a county. Arrest rates use 2020 census populations from the Maryland Department of Planning and leave out arrests at ICE's Baltimore offices, which people come to from across the state. The Eastern Shore includes Caroline, Cecil, Dorchester, Kent, Queen Anne's, Somerset, Talbot, Wicomico and Worcester counties. Map circles are placed at the center of each city.

Detainers

In the detainers file, each row is one detainer: a request from ICE that a jail or prison hold a person or notify the agency before releasing them. We kept detainers sent to facilities in Maryland (facility_state) and dated them by detainer_prepare_date. ICE does not say what kind of facility each one is, so CNS sorted the names in detention_facility into county jails, state prisons and state-run jails, federal custody, police or courts, and ICE offices, using keywords in the names. How a detainer ended comes from detainer_lift_reason; "Booked into Detention" means ICE took the person into custody. Detainers closed as duplicates or replaced by a new form were left out of those outcome shares.

Detention and departures

The project's file linking arrests to detention has one row per arrest, with the person's ICE detention stay attached. Hold room times come from book_in_date_time_first and book_out_date_time_first for people whose first stop (detention_facility_first) was the Baltimore hold room: the hours between the two, summarized as a monthly median by the month of book-in, in Eastern time. People detained since Jan. 20, 2025, are those with has_detention_stay; where they spent the longest part of their detention comes from detention_facility_state_longest. A recorded departure from the country is a non-missing departed_date, which includes removals and voluntary departures. Release and continued detention come from stay_release_reason_simple.

A person was counted as sent to a country other than their own when departure_country differs from citizenship_country; one record listing "KOREA" and "SOUTH KOREA" was treated as the same country. Departure counts cover people arrested since Jan. 20, 2025. These fields come from this linked file, which draws on ICE's detention and removal records; across all the records, the arrests file has slightly fewer departures (7,536 compared with 7,611). Globe circles are placed at the center of each country, not the arrival point.

Facilities and paths

In the detention stints file, each row is one stop at one facility within a person's detention stay. We kept the stops for the stays above, matched on stay_ID. The number of facilities a person passed through counts distinct detention_facility_code values; time in Louisiana and at Guantánamo Bay comes from the state column, where ICE codes Guantánamo as "CU." To trace paths, we put each person's stops in order of book_in_date_time and merged back-to-back stops at the same facility. The map shows the single most common path, followed by 360 people, and the most common path with five stops, followed by 25. Facilities are placed at the center of the city where they are located. Times on that route are medians for the group, measured from book_in_date_time to book_out_date_time; no individual's record is shown. Paths for people still detained when the records end may be incomplete.
